# Brad Harris — blog voice

A working voice definition drawn from the three recent posts in this repository. Use it for drafting and editing blog posts, not as a script to imitate word for word.

## Source posts

- `posts/articles/2023-05-09-dopamine-cdes.md`: personal experience leads to an unexpected technical connection; playful parentheticals; clear limits on the benefit claimed.
- `posts/articles/2022-10-07-farewell-slack.md`: concrete engineering stories, candid uncertainty, generous credit, short humorous observations inside longer narrative paragraphs.
- `posts/articles/2022-05-21-busted-hip-lessons.md`: scene before lesson; self-aware humor; reflection without pretending to have everything figured out.

## Core voice

An experienced builder sharing something he's noticed with peers. Warm, conversational, curious, and candid. Explain how you got to an idea and why it matters in actual work. Let the reader think along with you.

## How the writing moves

1. Start with an experience, familiar situation, or observation. Don't invent an anecdote to achieve this.
2. Explain the connection or surprise in ordinary language. Technical specifics should make the observation tangible.
3. Share what changed for you, including the limits of what you know.
4. Finish with a personal implication or an open-ended reflection. Avoid a sales pitch or universal rule.

Use connected paragraphs with a mix of sentence lengths. A short sentence can deliver an aside or a realization; a whole article of punchy fragments won't sound like these posts. Headings name a topic or lesson, rather than trying to be slogans.

## Language and personality

- First-person observations: “I've found,” “I think,” “I didn't expect.” Use them where they distinguish experience from general claims, not as filler in every sentence.
- Natural contractions, concrete verbs, familiar technical vocabulary. Explain a concept through an example before piling on terminology.
- Occasional parenthetical humor or a self-aware aside. It should belong to the story. Don't manufacture incompetence or personal history for a joke.
- Emoji and informal spelling appear in the originals, but aren't the voice itself. Use sparingly, if at all. Preserve clarity; don't copy typos.
- Give collaborators credit when relevant. Stay candid about incomplete work or uncertainty.
- Let enthusiasm come from the specific benefit or surprise, rather than superlatives.

## Avoid

- Product-marketing framing, feature tours, calls to action, and polished manifesto language.
- Repeated “not X, but Y” constructions, rhetorical questions used as headings, and stacks of one-line declarations.
- Invented personal experiences, dialogue, measurements, or claims of improved performance beyond what Brad has supplied.
- Treating an observation as a proven universal result.
- Mimicking signature phrases or inserting jokes/emoji on a schedule.

## Example edit for this article

Too abstract: “The shape of the review team can follow the code and what needs checking.”

Closer: “If one of those roles starts covering too much, I can split it up again. I don't need to find enough work to justify another person on the team.”

## Current article's intent

Human role categories can become a lossy compression of what agent reviewers can do. Brad has found that many focused roles, each assigned relevant changes and specific rules, review faster and more consistently. Dispatch's repository supplies concrete examples; the article should stay about the idea, not promote the tool's UI or features.

## Editing pass

Read it aloud. Does it sound like Brad explaining something he's tried, or a product page explaining why a feature is valuable? Keep the concrete examples, qualify the experience honestly, and remove any aside that feels performed.
